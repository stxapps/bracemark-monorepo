# Bracemark source security review

Prepared 21 October 2026. The review is ongoing and its coverage is partial.

This report summarises an AI-assisted review of selected source security boundaries. It preserves historical finding decisions and reports the source status of each confirmed finding at [source revision 1310f66](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/tree/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e). It also covers what changed around the first release: the fixes made before it, and what the reviews after it found in the build that went to production. It is not a new whole-product audit or an assessment of the deployed service.

## Results and their limits

The saved decisions contain four historically confirmed findings, all rated low overall. The original decisions concern imported data and its effects on storage, sync, or access to the importing account’s links. They did not establish cross-account disclosure or a break in encryption. This does not establish that those other classes of vulnerability are absent.

Source fixes for all four are present, with regression tests, and all four are in the production build. Each closes the route by which new input arrived. None repairs data that was already stored, and none has been retested on a running build by this campaign. The section on the confirmed findings states what each fix does and what it leaves.

| Latest saved decision | Distinct records | Meaning                                                                                                                |
| --------------------- | ---------------: | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Confirmed             |                4 | Historical source-review findings with a recorded consequence and an overall severity of low                           |
| Needs validation      |               19 | Source-grounded hypotheses whose decisive runtime, deployment, or policy facts remain unresolved; no severity assigned |
| Rejected              |                5 | The particular proposed security claim was not supported by its review; no severity assigned                           |

These 28 records are decisions about particular claims. Related routes can have separate records and different certainty. The counts are neither a count of currently open vulnerabilities nor a measure of product coverage. A fix does not change a record’s historical decision, so the four remain counted as confirmed, and a needs-validation record with a source fix stays a needs-validation record. This report does not independently re-decide every record against today’s source.

Reviews of changed source and rechecks of our own fixes up to 15 October raised seven further hypotheses as side observations. One was reviewed independently and is the nineteenth needs-validation record. The other six have no independent decision and no severity, and they are not in the table. Four of the six now have a source change that closes the path the hypothesis describes, each rechecked by a separate agent. The other two are as they were. The reviews since then have reported what they found as engineering problems with a stated consequence, not as records; the two that concern the production build are described under the first release.

No medium, high, or critical overall severity was assigned in these saved decisions. Unresolved hypotheses could change that picture after validation. Describing the result as “no vulnerabilities found” would omit the confirmed findings and the unfinished work.

## Method and attribution

We adapted [Cloudflare’s security-audit skill](https://github.com/cloudflare/security-audit-skill) for a sequence of focused source reviews. It supplied evidence standards, trust-boundary questions, candidate verification, and explicit treatment of missing evidence. Cloudflare did not conduct this review or certify Bracemark.

The original extractor-only pilot completed its scoped source-review workflow with partial coverage. Subsequent work used focused assignments, including mapping, hunting, verification, changed-source checks, engineering handoffs, and synthesis. Each assignment was not a separate execution of the skill’s full six-phase audit workflow.

The recorded plan requested GPT-6.1 Sol, with high reasoning for substantive security review and verification and medium reasoning for mapping and bookkeeping. The history also records an Astra/high pilot, Claude Opus 5.5 work, and sessions identified only as the active Codex runtime. Requested model and effort settings were not independently observable in every session. We therefore do not attribute the whole campaign to one model or treat a model name as evidence of coverage or accuracy. The fixes, the rechecks of them, and every review since 12 October used Claude Opus 5.5.

Surviving security claims received review in a separate, fresh-context agent session. That separation helps challenge the first agent’s assumptions. It remains verification by AI agents within an owner-directed process, rather than an external professional audit.

The fixes were written by an AI agent under our direction, or by us, and then rechecked the same way: a separate agent, starting from the saved finding or the earlier review and the changed source, was asked whether the route still worked and was not told the expected answer. Those rechecks have found defects in our fixes more than once, and two of the reviews since the release found that a change of ours had reopened something; both are described below. That is the most checkable evidence in this report that the rechecks do something.

The campaign records more than 300 completed bounded tasks. A task can finish with its decisive question still blocked. Some tasks inspect a single boundary; others reconcile evidence or prepare a handoff. The count measures recorded work, not the proportion of the product that is secure.

## Evidence boundary

The review inspected selected source, relevant installed dependency source, repository configuration, design records, and retained source-review evidence. Its saved decisions rest on reading source: no reviewing agent ran application code, browser fixtures, emulators, or native-device checks to reach one. It did not probe production services or establish effective provider configuration.

Since 20 October a review may also run our own code where reading it cannot settle a question: the repository’s unit tests, small probes written against the same functions, and mutation tests, in a separate copy of the source on a development machine. It may not touch production, a production credential, or real users’ data. The reviews described under the first release used it, and this report says where they rely on it. A probe like that shows what a function does with a given input; it is not a test of a deployed service or of a device, and it is not a runtime retest of a saved finding. No saved finding has one yet.

The fixes themselves are ordinary engineering changes. They were linted, type-checked, and run against the repository’s unit tests, including new tests that fail without each fix. That is evidence that the changes do what their tests say. It is not a retest of a finding on a running app.

Some historical findings were confirmed from their source traces. That label preserves the original source-review decision; it does not mean their behavior was reproduced on a device or against a deployed build. Runtime-dependent hypotheses remain needs validation.

The last full reconciliation, on 15 October, compared every saved record’s cited files with the source prepared for the release and read every commit since 12 October against the records. No saved decision changed. A narrower read after the release covered the security-relevant changes since then; it was not a byte check of every cited file. Byte equality, where it holds, establishes unchanged files, not unchanged runtime conditions or a verified deployed release.

The first finding was recorded at source revision `e87f089045056713e236bac3db0814a6d1af7864`. The other three were recorded at `748de48125e17a966efa638e5a56c2e360839226`. “Fixed for new imports” is the claim each row supports. A fix at the point where a file is admitted says nothing about a record admitted before it, and a source recheck says nothing about a device. A backup restore is a separate route with its own needs-validation records; what the release changed there is under the first release below.

### Backup reference admission

The [shared backup classifier](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/data/backup.ts) checks referenced file identifiers against the shared path grammar before admitting the entity. Both the [web restore](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/web-react/src/data/import-all-data.ts) and the [mobile restore](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/expo-react/src/data/import-all-data.ts) use that classifier before collecting referenced files for writes. The same function has since gained a date check and an identifier check beside the gate.

This supports the narrower statement that the historical entry route is gated for new imports. It does not demonstrate repair of state admitted before the fix, isolation of already malformed queue entries, or correct behavior on a running device. Recovery and retesting remain separate obligations.

### Associated files from refused rows

The [shared import projection](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/import/project.ts) used to choose every row’s image file in a first pass and validate the links in a second, and both platform importers write files before metadata. A row the schema refused therefore left its file behind.

The projection now chooses a file inside the same pass that validates its link, and records it only once the link is accepted. An image shared by a refused row and an accepted one is kept once, for the accepted row, in either order. The recheck traced both importers and found no other writer of these files during an import, and no later step that drops an accepted link while keeping its file.

What this leaves: files stranded by an import run before the fix are not collected, because removing them safely needs a complete view of every reference across devices; an import interrupted between its file writes and its link writes can still leave a file with no link; and a refused row can still leave behind an empty list or tag that it named. Retained-byte effects were not measured.

### Calendar admission and readers

The [entity schemas](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/sync/entities.ts) accept any safe integer as a timestamp, which is a larger range than a date can represent. One [predicate](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/sync/calendar-time.ts) asks whether a value is a representable date. It is applied at the two places a file is admitted, and by the readers that turn a date into text: the date shown in the web and mobile link lists and the [CSV serializer](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/export/csv.ts).

For an import from another app, the projection drops such a date and keeps the row, dated as an undated row would be. For a backup, the classifier refuses the line and counts it as invalid: a restore promises the values it admits unchanged, so it does not substitute a date. The readers no longer throw on a value that is already stored. The schemas were deliberately not tightened, since they also decode stored records and a stricter rule would make an existing link unreadable.

The first version of the backup check was wrong in one respect, and the recheck caught it: it tested an extraction timestamp on the wrong object, so that field was not checked at all, and the settings file’s timestamps were not checked either. A check of the corrected version then found that a deliberately malformed line could make it raise an error and stop the whole restore, where a bad line should only be counted. Both are corrected, and a later recheck by a separate agent found no timestamp the schemas define left unchecked, no line that can make the classifier raise, and no other reader in the web app, the mobile app, or the browser extension that throws on such a value.

What this leaves: a value already stored, or arriving from another device, is tolerated and not repaired, and the app has no way to change a link’s creation date. Refusing has a cost of its own: a backup exported from an account that already holds such a date will lose that link when restored, reported in the count of entries that could not be read. A dropped date in an import from another app is not reported at all. The separate backup-route hypothesis keeps its unresolved decision; a source fix does not promote or close it.

### List structure from another app

An import from another app turns each level of a file’s folder path into a list. The confirmed finding was that a file could describe nesting deep enough that the code reading the list tree, which used a level of the call stack for each level of the tree, could fail on every signed-in screen.

Two things changed. [Building the tree and every walk over it](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/sync/tree.ts), [list-lock coverage](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/sync/lock-coverage.ts), the export serializers, and the sidebar no longer use one call-stack frame per level, so a deep tree that sync delivers is read without one. Lock coverage reads the same tree as before, not a shortened copy, so a deep list stays covered by a lock above it. And an import from another app no longer creates a list past [100 levels](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/import/resolve.ts). The first version of that limit refused the whole file. Since the release it places a deeper folder at the 100th level instead, so the import keeps every link and loses only the nesting below that level.

The recheck caught two defects in the first version: the sidebar on web and mobile still nested once per level, and the depth check ran after the prompt that offers to import only what fits a plan. Both were corrected. A later recheck by a separate agent, after the [LinkWarden reader](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/import/linkwarden.ts) and the list pickers changed, found that no import from another app creates a chain deeper than the limit for any shape of file it could construct. It also found that a LinkWarden file whose collections form a loop of parents is cut in one place that depends on the order of the file, and that lists nested more than 32 levels deep can show the same label in a picker. We kept both and wrote them down. The review of the replaced rule in October found the import still held to the limit across the whole library.

What this leaves: sync can still deliver a tree of any depth, by design, because two devices can each make a legal move that together nest past the limit; reading a deeply nested browser bookmarks file costs time and memory before the limit is checked; and there is no recovery screen that works independently of the list tree. Device failure thresholds, cross-device propagation, and actual interruption and recovery behavior remain unmeasured.

## Before the first release

Three needs-validation records gained a source fix before the first release, and all three are in the production build. Each fix was rechecked by a separate agent, and none changes the record’s decision.

**A synced file is bound to its storage path.** Each encrypted file is opened with the account’s data key, which proves the file is the account’s, but nothing tied a file to the path it is stored under. Whoever answered a request could therefore hand back another of the account’s own files, one link’s record in place of another’s, and it would open. Every file is now sealed with its sync path as [associated data](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/crypto/blob-aad.ts), on web and mobile and in the iOS and Android native code, all held to one shared test vector. A file served under another path fails to open. It does not stop a server from serving an older version of the same path, leaving paths out, or withholding updates; [the design record](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/docs/local-first-sync.md) states those limits. We made this change before the first release, because changing the file format afterwards needs a migration of every account.

**A draft from the iOS share extension is bound to its account.** A link shared under one account could, if the account changed before the app took the draft in, be saved under the next one. The share sheet now stamps each draft with the account it was made under, and the app saves it only under that account; a draft from another account is refused, and then deleted. The native race is not prevented, only its outcome. The process lifetimes involved have not been observed on a device.

**A restored list or tag is read under one identifier.** A backup could carry several lists, or several tags, that each claimed the same identifier under different storage paths. The code that builds the list tree gave every copy its own copy of everything nested beneath it, so a small file could describe a very large tree. [The restore refuses](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/data/backup.ts) a list, tag, or pin whose identifier is not the one in its storage path; both apps read each [from that path](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/sync/items.ts); and the tree holds one entry per identifier. A record already stored with two identifiers that disagree is read under the one in its path and is not repaired; a lock set on such a list on a device no longer covers it. No build we released can write one.

The values the app can no longer change without migrating live accounts — key derivation, key wrapping, the recovery code, signed proofs, and the encrypted file format — were read once more before the release, on both platforms and in the native file code. That reading found no defect in them. It was done by the session that made the fixes, not by a separate agent.

## The first release, and what the reviews since found

As of 21 October, the production web app and API run builds from 18 October, and the mobile apps and browser extensions are built from a commit of the same day. Those builds carry every fix described above.

They also carry three changes we made in the week before the release, and the reviews after it read each against the earlier results.

**A file that fails to open is skipped.** Before, a synced file that failed its integrity check stopped sync with an error until it was resolved. Now the app logs it, stores nothing for it, and moves on. A separate review found that the binding to the storage path still holds: no route opens a skipped file, or opens one under another path, and a server gains nothing it could not already do by withholding a file. What the change costs is detection. A damaged file or a bug of ours now becomes a link quietly missing on that device, while sync reports itself up to date, and the review found one place where that did harm. In the web app, a cleanup that runs once per session deletes a link’s saved preview and its image when the link is not in the local store, and queues that delete for every device. With the skip, a link that was skipped on one device was absent there, so the cleanup deleted its preview everywhere. We observed that by running the web sync code and the cleanup together in a test harness. The consequence is loss of preview data in the account’s own library, low in severity.

[The fix](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/web-react/src/data/sweep-dangling-extractions.ts) records each path the pull skipped and has the cleanup pass over a link that is recorded, and it runs the cleanup only at the end of a sync cycle that succeeded, while that cycle still holds its lock, so no pull is half done. A separate agent reviewed it before it was committed and found it closes those routes and breaks nothing it could find. It also found four older ways a link can be missing on one device and present on the server — among them a skip made by a build from before the record existed — and the cleanup still cannot tell those apart. The committed version differs from the reviewed one only in comments, design notes, and tests, which now list those gaps. **The fix is committed and not yet deployed.**

**Restoring a backup places deep lists instead of refusing.** Shortly before the release, a restore refused a backup whose lists or tags nested past 100 levels. The release places each line past the limit under its ancestor on the 99th level instead, and the list and tag editors refuse a move past 100. A separate review found that this holds every tree a restore stores to 100 levels, within the file. It also found that it had reopened the route through a loop. The restore measured a loop of parents the way the screens show it, cut in one place, and stored the file’s parents as they were written. A backup with a short loop and long chains hanging off it, arranged so the cut detached each chain, was therefore stored at 99 levels with nothing placed, and one ordinary edit afterwards — moving the loop’s top row up — removed the loop and joined the chains into one as deep as the file was long. The editors’ rule measured the tree as drawn, so it could not see that either. The review showed this by running the restore’s shared functions and the edit in a probe, which produced a chain of 19,802 levels from a file of that many lines; neither app’s restore was run end to end. The consequence it established is a broken limit in the importing account’s own tree, synced to its devices, with display cost. No crash was shown, and the rule we replaced refused such a file.

[The fix](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/shared/src/data/backup.ts) stores the cut, so a line the loop’s cut made a top-level list is restored as one, and the [move itself](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo/blob/1310f66c8ae261fe23d4b6e3987f02c5ca84581e/packages/web-react/src/hooks/use-list-mutations.ts) now measures the stored tree after the move, on web and mobile, for lists and tags. A separate agent rechecked the fix on 21 October, at the source and by running the restore’s shared functions, the move check, and mutated copies of both in a separate copy of the source. It found that the fix holds for that route. After a restore, the stored tree holds no parent the screens ignore other than a missing one or Trash, the earlier probe stays at 99 levels after the moves that released it, and no single move it tried after a restore — about 31,000 for each kind of tree — went past 100 levels. The move check never let a list or tag end deeper than 100 levels, or deeper than it already sat, across thousands of random moves, and deleting a member of a loop is refused while anything names it as a parent. It also found four things, all older than the fix: a loop can still be built across two or more restores, where the first file names a parent only the second supplies, and the move check is then the only guard against releasing it; restores still stack, as below; renaming or reordering a list writes the parent it was drawn with, without the check; and a move can make a list the child of its own descendant, which stores a loop, though not a deeper tree. The tests pin the restore and the list move, and nothing pins the same check on the tag move. **The fix is committed and not yet deployed.**

What this leaves: the limit holds per file, not per library. A restore measures only the file’s own lines, and a list the account already holds keeps its stored parent, so a restore can hang a file’s chains under deep lists the account holds and restores add up: in the recheck’s probe, two restores stored a tree 396 levels deep with nothing placed. The rule we replaced measured the same way.

**A second way to transfer files.** Files can now go to and from storage through our API — the service behind sign-in, sync, and billing — under a short-lived token for one path, as well as through the storage provider’s own signed addresses. A separate review read it before it was deployed and found that the authorization holds on both routes. It found two gaps in the check that ties an upload to the commit that records it. Both affect only the account’s own storage accounting, and we treat them as engineering work. This change is committed and not yet deployed, and the apps in the stores use the earlier route only.

## Unresolved work

The 19 needs-validation records are not low-confidence confirmed vulnerabilities. They remain unresolved because a specific fact could change the decision. None was decided again. Several now have a source change that closes the route they describe for new input — the three above, the backup route for dates, and part of one account-switch hypothesis in the web app — and others have new conditions that a runtime check would have to account for. A source change does not settle a needs-validation record; the fact that would settle it is still missing.

This public summary groups the missing evidence without publishing reproduction material:

- Effective preview-service network and resource controls, including the relationship between application checks and runtime or provider enforcement.
- Actual session, storage, and sync ordering, with concrete consequences for a protected resource rather than a hypothetical race alone.
- Native and browser lifecycle behavior when previously admitted work survives account changes or cancellation, including what a real iOS or Android build reports for a file that fails to open.
- Actual acceptance, reader effects, and recovery for related backup and structural-input hypotheses, including whether a backup someone else wrote is an input the restore must defend against.
- Product authority and observed outbound behavior around restored settings and preview consent.

Rejected claims remain separate from engineering observations. A correctness problem affecting only the same owner is not automatically a security finding, and rejection of a particular claim does not establish that its entire subsystem is safe.

## Next evidence

Most of the facts above are reachable today: our own code run on a development machine against a local API with test accounts, and a separate staging deployment with its own storage and its own test accounts. Neither touches production or real users’ data. What neither reaches is production itself, a signed build on a physical device, and a provider’s own policy, and those stay named as gaps. Repeating nearby source questions cannot settle device lifetime, provider behavior, or actual concurrent outcomes.

Evidence of that kind should establish a minimal effect with synthetic accounts and data, test competing controls and recovery, and stop at the bounded result. Findings whose source has changed need an updated dependency trace before testing. The six undecided hypotheses each need a decision or a fix of their own, and each fix needs its own recheck. Any resulting decision, fix check, or retest should state its revision, platform, scope, and remaining limitations.

No completion date is asserted here. This work is not an external professional audit, and we have not commissioned one.

The [source repository](https://github.com/stxapps/bracemark-monorepo) is public. You can read the mechanisms described here and the exact source snapshot linked above.
